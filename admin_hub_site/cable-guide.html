cable-guide

Which cable for 10 Gbit, why the new access point reboots at the end of a long run, and what the colours on a fibre mean. The tables to look things up, one drawing to wire a plug, and a calculator for voltage drop.

Cat 825 and 40 Gbit, only to 30 minside a data centre row
  • The category is printed on the jacket. A link is as good as its worst part: Cat 6A cable with Cat 5e sockets is a Cat 5e link.
  • 100 m means 90 m of fixed cable plus 10 m of patch cables in total.
  • CCA (copper-clad aluminium) is not a category. It breaks, heats up under PoE and fails certification. Buy solid copper for fixed runs.
  • Stranded cable for patch cords, solid cable in the wall. Each needs its own kind of plug.
  • New installations: Cat 6A. It carries 10 Gbit and the high PoE classes without getting warm in bundles.

wiring an RJ45 plug

  • Untwist as little as possible, about 1 cm. The twist is what makes the cable work.
  • The jacket must reach into the plug, under the strain relief.
  • Test every hand-made cable. A split pair passes a simple continuity check and fails at gigabit.
  • Crossover cables are history: every port since gigabit sorts it out itself.

  • Multimode and single-mode do not mix: the transceivers on both ends and the fibre between must match.
  • Connector colour: blue is single-mode with a flat polish (UPC), green is angled (APC). Never plug green into blue: it damages both.
  • LC is the small duplex plug on nearly every SFP. SC is the larger square one. MPO carries 8 or 12 fibres for 40 and 100 Gbit.
  • Do not look into a fibre or a port. Keep the dust caps on: one grain of dust is a bad link.
  • Bend gently: no tighter than a coffee mug.

  • Switches check the vendor code of what you plug in. A cable coded for one maker may be refused by another: order it coded for both ends.

voltage drop

A long, thin cable loses voltage on the way. PoE devices at the end of 90 m, door stations and 12 V cameras are where it shows: the device restarts under load.

rules for every cable

  • Label both ends, the same on both, before the cable goes in.
  • Data cables cross power cables at a right angle and do not run alongside them.
  • Hook-and-loop ties, hand tight. A cable tie pulled hard changes the cable's geometry and its speed.
  • Leave a little slack at both ends. The next person has to re-terminate it once.
  • Outdoor runs need outdoor cable, and copper between two buildings needs surge protection, or better: fibre.
  • Patch cables in three or four lengths and one colour per purpose beat a drawer of random ones.

Speeds and distances are those of the standards; a good installation often does more, a bad one less. The voltage drop is worked out for copper at 20 °C with the current the device draws at the lowered voltage, without connector losses: treat the result as the best case. PoE standards expect at most 12.5 ohms per pair loop on 100 m.
